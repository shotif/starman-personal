# *Mass Effect*: how it works, what worked, what failed

Research for the first round of [decisions](../DECISIONS.md), 24 October 2026. Mechanics and
structure only. Starman Lightkeeper uses no names, text, art or data from these games (§6).

Most of what follows describes how the games play and comes from the games themselves. Claims
about reception and development cite the sources listed in §7.

## 1. The series

| Game | Year | In short |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Mass Effect* | 2007 | An RPG with shooting: stats affected accuracy, open planets explored by vehicle, a long, deep main story |
| *Mass Effect 2* | 2010 | A cover shooter with RPG systems: tighter combat, a crew of twelve, personal missions, and a finale built from your preparation. Usually counted the best |
| *Mass Effect 3* | 2012 | The best combat of the trilogy, power combos, heavy melee; an ending widely criticised for ignoring the player's choices (later reworked by the studio) |
| *Mass Effect: Andromeda* | 2017 | A new hero in a new galaxy: very mobile combat (jump jets, dashes) but weak writing, many errands and troubled development |
| *Legendary Edition* | 2021 | The trilogy remastered |
| Next game | — | In development; in November 2025 the studio confirmed work continues, with no date [5][6] |

## 2. How it works

### 2.1 The hero

- **One hero**, named by the player, man or woman, with a face built in an editor. In *Andromeda*
  the hero has a twin, played or not.
- **A chosen past**, in two parts: where they grew up (on ships, on a colony, or on Earth) and the
  defining event of their career (sole survivor of a disaster, a war hero, or ruthless). Each
  choice adds a few lines, a small mission, and a small bonus.
- **Six classes from three disciplines**: combat (weapons and armour), tech (drones, hacking,
  overloads) and a psychic-like discipline. Three pure classes and three hybrids. The class decides
  your powers and which weapons you are trained in. *Andromeda* dropped classes for free respecs
  between "profiles".

### 2.2 Combat

- **Real time, third person, with cover**, from the second game on: snap to cover, pop out to
  shoot. Reviewers liked the feel but noted unwanted snapping in and out of cover, and arenas
  "telegraphed" by waist-high walls [2].
- **Defences in layers**: shields, barriers and armour, each weak to certain weapons or powers, so
  a squad's mix matters.
- **Powers on cooldowns**. The second game used one shared cooldown [2]. The third added
  **combos**: one power primes, another detonates. It also made weight matter: carrying more guns
  slows your powers.
- **The pause**: the player can stop time at any moment to aim powers and give the squad orders
  [1]. On a controller, quick buttons direct each companion [2].
- **Squad orders are simple**: move there, attack that, use this power, regroup. Companions fight
  on their own between orders.
- **Melee** is a quick strike, and in the third game a class-specific heavy blow. It is never a
  real system.
- **Andromeda's combat** is the most mobile and often called the series' best: jump jets, dashes,
  cover that comes and goes as you move. But you cannot aim companions' powers.

### 2.3 Dialogue and morality

- **The wheel**: choices on a ring, shown as short paraphrases. One side moves the conversation on
  and the other asks questions. The top is the kind option and the bottom the hard one. When the
  paraphrase and the spoken line differed, players were often surprised by what the hero said.
- **A two-way meter**: kind and ruthless points. High scores unlock persuasion options, so players
  were pushed to pick one side all game to have the best options at the end. *Andromeda* replaced
  this with tones (emotional, logical, casual, professional) and no meter.
- **Interrupts**: quick prompts during scenes to act on impulse (shoot, push, comfort). Much loved.

### 2.4 Companions

- Six in the first game, twelve in the second (with extras), about seven in the third. **Two at a
  time** go with you.
- **Personal missions** (the second game's "loyalty missions") settle each companion's story. Loyal
  companions are likelier to live [3].
- **Life on the ship**: between missions you walk the decks, talk to each companion, and some
  become romances.
- **They can die**, for good, through choices in the first game and in the second game's finale.

### 2.5 The finale of the second game

The finale is the series' most praised design. You assign companions to roles (a tech expert for
the vents, a leader for the second team, a defender for the barrier). Survival depends on ship
upgrades you bought, which companions are loyal, whom you chose for each role, and how long you
waited before going, while a captured crew is lost bit by bit [3][4]. **Everything you did becomes
the ending.** Two criticisms: once the rules are known, saving everyone is easy [8], and the game
otherwise rarely punishes doing things in any order [4].

### 2.6 Structure

- **A hub** (a great station) full of people and side stories; **main missions** that can often be
  done in any order; **side missions** on other worlds.
- The first game's side worlds were explored by vehicle, with repeated bases. The second game's side
  missions were short and authored. Its **planet scanning** for resources was widely felt to be
  tedious.
- The third game tied side content to the ending through a single score of "war readiness".
- **Choices carry from game to game** through imported saves.
- *Andromeda* returned to open planets with outposts and vehicles. Its development cut a planned
  procedurally generated galaxy, from many worlds down to seven [7]. It was rushed, and was
  criticised for bugs, uneven writing and animation [7].

## 3. What worked

1. **The crew.** Companions with their own stories, opinions and missions, and scenes between them
   and the hero, are what players remember most.
2. **The hero as yours.** Creating the hero and choosing their past made the story personal, even
   though the past changed little.
3. **The ship as home.** The space between missions, where the crew lives, gives the story its
   rhythm.
4. **A finale built from preparation.** The second game turned the whole campaign into the ending.
5. **Combat with roles.** Defences and powers made the squad's make-up a choice, not decoration.
6. **Interrupts.** Acting in the moment, inside a scene.
7. **Consequences across the whole story**, even across games.

## 4. What failed

1. **The meter.** Points that unlock options reward picking one side, not thinking.
2. **Paraphrases.** The hero saying something other than what the player chose.
3. **Filler.** Repeated bases, scanning, errands, and lists of tasks (*Andromeda*).
4. **An ending that ignored the journey** (the third game, as first released).
5. **Empty open spaces.** Large planets with little to do.
6. **Thin melee**, and companions you can only point at targets.
7. **Repeated arenas** that warn you a fight is coming [2].

## 5. Lessons for Starman Lightkeeper

| Lesson | How it shapes us |
| --- | --- |
| The crew is the heart | Fewer companions, each with a recruitment story, a personal story, and opinions on what you do (D-14) |
| The past should matter | A backstory that opens its own quest and changes how people treat you (D-11) |
| No meter to farm | Choices remembered by people, not counted by a score (D-12) |
| Show the real line | Full lines in the dialogue list (D-13) |
| Preparation is the ending | A finale decided by whom you won over, what you learned and what you built |
| No filler | Hand-made places only; every side quest a story (D-19) |
| Orders, not puppets | Companions take objectives for the area, in fights and outside them (D-16) |
| Melee as an equal | A real melee system with parries and combos beside the guns (D-17) |
| Phones change controls | Assisted aim, a command view one tap away, cover by moving into it (D-17) |

## 6. What we must not use

No names, terms or designs that belong to *Mass Effect*, including: its species, its races' names,
its organisations and ranks, its ship's name, its hub station, its villains and their cycle, the
"relay" network that links its galaxy, its rare element, its psychic-like discipline and its name,
its class names, its morality terms, its multi-tool's name, and its loyalty-mission and
suicide-mission names. Structures (six classes from three disciplines, a finale built from
preparation) are general design patterns. We use them with our own content, names and reasons.
